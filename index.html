Briefing · 01

Dr.
StrangeloveOr: How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Bomb
Stanley Kubrick · USA · 1964 · 95 min

An American general decides, entirely on his own, to start a nuclear war with the Soviet Union. Then he locks himself in his office and cuts the phone lines, so nobody can tell the bombers to turn around.

The rest of the film takes place in three rooms, and the people in them have about ninety minutes to stop the end of the world. One of them is drunk. One of them keeps suggesting that, since the war has started anyway, they may as well finish the job properly.

It is a comedy. It was released in January 1964 — fifteen months after the Cuban Missile Crisis, when this very nearly happened for real.

Directed by Stanley Kubrick · written with Terry Southern
From the deadly serious thriller "Red Alert" by Peter George

Briefing · 02 · the world outside

Nineteen years from Hiroshima to this film

To see why a comedy about nuclear war was shocking, you need to know how fast things had moved. In 1945 exactly one country on Earth had the bomb. By 1964 two of them had enough to end civilisation several times over, aimed at each other permanently, with bombers in the air around the clock.

Quiz
The US Air Force refused to help with the film. What did they insist on instead?
The film opens with a caption insisting that real safeguards would prevent anything like this. Think about what that tells you: the Air Force was worried enough to demand a denial. Nobody bothers denying something the public would find ridiculous. Watch for that caption tonight — it is the first joke in the film, and it was not written as one.
Briefing · 03 · the director

A chess hustler from the Bronx

Stanley Kubrick never went to film school. He grew up in New York, was a famously mediocre student, and spent his teenage afternoons playing chess for money in Washington Square Park — which is arguably better training in reading an opponent than most film courses.

At seventeen he sold a photograph to Look magazine and was hired onto the staff. He spent five years learning to tell a story in a single frame, then taught himself to direct by simply going and doing it.

By 1964 he was thirty-five, with Paths of Glory, Spartacus and Lolita already behind him. Dr. Strangelove brought him his first three Oscar nominations.

What came afterwards is why the name matters: 2001: A Space Odyssey, A Clockwork Orange, The Shining, Full Metal Jacket. In forty-six years he made thirteen feature films — about one every three and a half years — because he controlled every part of them and refused to be hurried.

MethodResearch firstTAP Before writing a word of this film he read his way through the literature of nuclear strategy — by most accounts dozens of books. He ended up understanding the subject better than many of the journalists covering it.
MethodTake after takeTAP He became famous, and in some quarters infamous, for shooting a scene over and over. Some actors found it freeing. Others found it unbearable — and you are about to meet one of those.
MethodControl everythingTAP Camera, cutting, sound, posters, even the rules cinemas had to follow when projecting his films. He moved to England in the early 1960s and made almost everything from there, well out of reach of Hollywood supervision.
Worth chewing on
Thirteen films in forty-six years.

Directors of comparable standing often made three or four times as many. Is making very few things very carefully a strength, or a kind of waste? There is a real argument on both sides.

Briefing · 03 · structure

The whole film happens in three rooms

And here's the thing that makes it unbearable: the three rooms can barely talk to each other.

Kubrick cuts between them for ninety minutes. Everyone is trying to fix the same problem. Nobody has all the information. Every room thinks the other rooms are being stupid. The telephone becomes the most frightening object in the film.

Pick a room and we'll go in. The indicator at the top shows where you are.

Before we go in
Three rooms, one problem, no shared picture.

Where else do you see this shape? A group of people who all want the same outcome, all have a piece of the truth, and cannot get it into one place fast enough. It's not only a Cold War problem.

Room 01 · Burpelson Air Base

Five links, and the world ends

General Jack D. Ripper is convinced the Soviets are poisoning Americans through the water supply. So he issues the attack order himself.

That only works because five separate things are true at once. Tap each link. Your job: find the one that, if removed, stops everything.

Link 01 A general can issue the attack order without the President. In the film it's "Plan R", designed so the US can still strike back if Washington has already been destroyed. And in reality? Declassified documents show that authority to use nuclear weapons had, in certain circumstances, genuinely been delegated down to military commanders — for exactly that reason. The scariest link is the real one.
Link 02 Only he knows the recall code. Three letters, in his head, nowhere else. A security feature that works beautifully right up until the man holding it is the problem. Every safety system in this film fails the same way: it was designed against an enemy outside, never against someone inside.
Link 03 He seals the base and cuts all communication. Standard procedure once an attack is under way — nobody can phone in and impersonate command. It also means nobody can phone in and tell the truth.
Link 04 The bombers' radios can't receive anything but the code. A device called the CRM-114 blocks every transmission that doesn't start with the right three letters. Designed to stop the enemy from faking a recall. It also stops your own President.
Link 05 The Soviets have built a machine that fires automatically. The Doomsday Machine, which detonates on its own if the USSR is hit, and cannot be switched off — because a system you can switch off doesn't frighten anyone. More on this in Room 03, and there's a very unpleasant surprise waiting there.
Room 01 · the man himself

The general's reason is deliberately insane

Ripper doesn't want power, money or glory. He believes fluoride in American drinking water is a Soviet plot to weaken people from the inside, and he has reached this conclusion through a chain of reasoning that is internally perfect and completely mad.

Kubrick could have made him a traitor, a lunatic with a grudge, or a man blackmailed by Moscow. He chose something much more uncomfortable: a sincere patriot with a conspiracy theory and the authority to act on it.

Quiz
Why is that a stronger choice?
Every security system in the world was built to catch spies, bribery and blackmail. None of them was built to catch a loyal officer with a theory. Ripper passes every check, because he isn't lying about anything. That's why the film is still frightening sixty years later — the hole it points at has never been closed.
Worth chewing on
Follow the logic backwards.

Ripper's conclusion is deranged. His reasoning, step by step, is almost valid. Where exactly does it break? Finding that spot is a genuinely useful skill, and it works on arguments far less obviously mad than his.

Room 02 · the B-52

The only people doing their jobs properly

Up in the bomber, nobody is mad, corrupt or stupid. Major Kong and his crew are brave, competent, loyal and calm under pressure. They check their procedures. They look after each other. When the plane is damaged, they improvise brilliantly.

And every single thing they do correctly brings the end of the world closer.

Casting accidentHe wasn't meant to be thereTAP Peter Sellers was supposed to play four roles including Kong. He couldn't manage the Texas accent, and an ankle injury settled it. Slim Pickens — a real former rodeo cowboy — got the part instead.
MethodNobody told himTAP By a much-repeated account, Pickens was never told he was in a comedy, and played it entirely straight. Whether or not that's exactly true, it's why the performance works — he isn't winking at you.
The cockpitToo accurateTAP The Air Force wouldn't help, so the designers reconstructed a B-52 interior from published photographs and guesswork. It reportedly came out so close to the real thing that people wondered how they'd done it.
The real question
Who is to blame on that plane?

Nobody disobeys. Nobody is cruel. Everyone is excellent at their job. If the crew are blameless and the world still ends, where exactly does the fault live?

Room 02 · the image

A cowboy rides a nuclear weapon

You have almost certainly met this picture before. It has escaped the film completely — it turns up on posters, album covers and T-shirts, and most people who recognise it have never watched the movie it comes from. So let's look at it as an image, and leave where it falls in the story for tonight.

Kubrick dresses an American nuclear bomb like a bucking bronco and puts a cowboy on it, hat in the air. It works because it collapses two things into one picture: the American frontier myth — the rodeo, the lone rider, the cheerful conquest of something dangerous — and the most destructive object humans have ever built. One gesture, and the film says: this is the same spirit, pointed at the end of the world.

You've seen this argument before in this course, made with a balloon. Chaplin gave a dictator a globe and let him dance. Kubrick gives a pilot a bomb and lets him ride it. Both scenes are wordless. Both are about a man who is having a wonderful time.

And notice the warheads are stencilled with cheerful handling instructions, as if they were furniture.

A joke is an argument that you can't look away from.

Room 03 · the War Room

The most famous room that never existed

There is no War Room in the White House. Ken Adam invented this one — a vast dark triangle, a circular table under a ring of light, and a wall of maps that dwarfs everybody in front of it.

Adam had just designed the villain's lair in the first James Bond film. Kubrick hired him off the back of it, then argued with him for months.

The tableCovered in green feltTAP Kubrick insisted on it so it would read as a poker table — these men are gambling with the planet. The film is black and white. No audience could ever see the colour. It was there for the actors, so they'd feel it.
The scaleEveryone is tinyTAP The Big Board towers over the men deciding things. Shot after shot shrinks them against the machinery they're supposed to be controlling. The set is making the argument before anyone opens their mouth.
The afterlifeIt became the real thingTAP This invented room has shaped how everyone pictures nuclear command ever since — in films, television and the public imagination. There's a famous story that a newly elected US president asked to be shown the War Room, and was told it didn't exist.
Room 03 · the machine

A machine built to be impossible to stop

Halfway through, the Soviet ambassador reveals that the USSR has built a Doomsday Machine: buried bombs that detonate automatically if the country is attacked, destroying all human life.

The President asks the obvious question — why would you build such a thing? And the answer is the cleverest and nastiest idea in the film: a deterrent only works if it cannot be switched off. If a human can stop it, the enemy will gamble that the human loses their nerve. So you remove the human.

Then the second question lands, and it's worse. Why didn't they tell anyone? A secret doomsday machine deters precisely nobody.

This isn't Kubrick being clever for fun. The idea came from real strategic thinking of the period — the men paid to reason about nuclear war had genuinely worked through this logic on paper.

The hard one
Is the Doomsday Machine stupid, or is it just honest?

It takes deterrence — the actual policy of both superpowers — and removes the comforting part, the bit where a person could still say no. Nothing else about it is different. So which is more frightening: the machine, or the policy it's an exaggeration of?

Room 03 · test

Film, or real life?

Kubrick's defence was always that he hadn't invented much. Six statements — decide which ones are inventions and which ones actually happened.

A sign reading "Peace is our Profession" stands at the air base where the war starts.
In real life, a US commander could, in some circumstances, order a nuclear strike without the President.
The White House War Room, as shown in the film, really exists.
Kubrick invented the Doomsday Machine concept from nothing.
The film's release was delayed, and a line re-recorded, because of the Kennedy assassination.
The USSR eventually built a semi-automatic system to guarantee retaliation after a decapitating strike.
Debrief · 01

The day Kubrick gave up on being serious

He did not set out to make a comedy. He bought Red Alert, a genuinely tense thriller, and began writing a faithful, serious adaptation of it.

And the more he read, the worse his problem got. Every time he wrote a scene straight, the real logic underneath turned out to be absurd on its own. Deterrence only works if your enemy believes you might be insane. A safety system that can be overridden isn't safe — and one that can't be overridden is terrifying. The serious version kept collapsing into farce without any help from him.

So he stopped fighting it, brought in the satirist Terry Southern, and rewrote the whole thing as a comedy. The joke wasn't a decision. It was a conclusion.

A detail worth knowing
One actor did not enjoy this film.

George C. Scott, who plays the general on the right of the poster, wanted to play it seriously. Kubrick persuaded him to do deliberately over-the-top "practice" takes, promising they would never be used — then put them in the finished film. Scott reportedly swore never to work with him again. Watch his performance tonight knowing that, and notice that it is also the funniest thing in the picture.

Quiz
What does that story tell you about satire?
Kubrick barely had to push. He took the real reasoning of nuclear strategy, followed it honestly to the end, and the laugh was already sitting there. That's why it still lands. You can't argue with it by saying it's unfair — it isn't exaggerating enough to be unfair.
Debrief · 02

Chaplin and Kubrick, same job, opposite endings

You've now watched two comedies about a catastrophe that was happening while the film was being made. Both by men who could have made something safe instead. Tap through the comparison.

The real question
Which ending is braver?

Chaplin stops his film and pleads with the audience for four minutes. Kubrick refuses to comfort you for even one second. One of those is harder to do. Decide which — and be ready to defend it next time.

Debrief · 03

Six things to catch tonight

Ninety-five minutes, black and white. It is genuinely very funny — let it be funny first.

  • Find all three Sellers. He plays an RAF officer, the President, and Strangelove. See how long it takes you to stop noticing it's the same man.
  • Watch George C. Scott's face when he isn't speaking. Kubrick got those enormous reactions out of him by a trick the actor was reportedly furious about afterwards.
  • Count the telephones. Seriously. The most dangerous object in this film is a phone that doesn't work properly.
  • Read the signs and the stencilling in the background — on the base, on the plane, on the bombs. There's a second film written in the set dressing.
  • Notice the camera on the plane versus the camera in the War Room. One is handheld and chaotic, one is still and enormous. Ask why.
  • The last two minutes. Listen to the song playing over the ending, then look up when it was written and what it's about. That's your homework thought.
